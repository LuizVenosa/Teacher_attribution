\begin{abstract}
Black-box distillation makes it possible to train student language models from teacher outputs, making model provenance difficult to verify from model cards alone.
This project asks whether modern representation learning can substantially improve teacher detection from outputs.
For each prompt, responses are generated from all candidate teachers and students, then a prompt-conditioned contrastive encoder pulls a student response toward its documented teacher and away from same-prompt alternatives.
Direct retrieval reaches 60.44\% single-prompt accuracy, while a frozen logistic-regression probe over the learned embedding reaches 63.12\% accuracy and 89.11\% macro ROC-AUC, slightly above the strongest replicated lexical baseline at 62.17\%.
Aggregating prompts strengthens the signal, reaching 97.5\% accuracy with eight prompts and 100\% with sixteen.
The results suggest that public students preserve detectable teacher fingerprints, although attribution remains task-dependent.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Knowledge distillation transfers capabilities from a large teacher language model into a smaller student \citep{xu2024surveykd}.
It improves efficiency, but black-box distillation also creates a provenance problem: a student can be trained from teacher responses without access to the teacher's weights or data \citep{chen2024blackboxkd}.
Recent public debates about whether open-source models distill from stronger closed-source systems motivated the question of this project: \textbf{can modern representation learning substantially improve teacher detection from model outputs?}
Reliable teacher attribution would help audit public model lineages and reveal which behavioral traces survive distillation, giving a better view of what is actually transferred between language models.

\citet{li-etal-2024-where} study origin attribution for LLM-generated text, while \citet{wadhwa-etal-2025-taught} focus on teacher tracing in controlled distillation.
This project adapts the question to a messier setting: public teacher-student model pairs whose model cards document a teacher or source model.

Public lineages are realistic but noisy: model cards vary, students may inherit from a family rather than one clean teacher, and checkpoints may include additional instruction tuning.
To reduce topic leakage, all candidate teachers and students answer the same prompts, producing examples with one student response, several same-prompt teacher responses, and a documented teacher label.

The modeling idea comes from contrastive authorship attribution \citep{ai-etal-2022-whodunit,hu2024contrastive,huertas2024isolating}: if authors leave stylistic traces separable from topic, teacher models may leave behavioral traces in their students.
The proposed model therefore contrasts each student response against same-prompt teacher responses.

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a reproducible public-lineage teacher-attribution pipeline;
    \item a prompt-conditioned contrastive encoder for same-prompt student-teacher comparison;
    \item an evaluation of whether the learned representation improves over lexical teacher-detection baselines.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Prompt Bank and Data}

The prompt bank follows \citet{wadhwa-etal-2025-taught}: summarization, question answering, and instruction following.
It draws from CNN/DailyMail, SumpubMed, Rotten Tomatoes, CommonsenseQA, OpenBookQA, QuaRel, and Alpaca-style instructions.
All teachers and students answer the same prompts.
After filtering, the run contains 29,742 training prompts and 3,000 test prompts.
Each prompt contributes one row per teacher label, so the test split has 12,000 balanced attribution rows with prompt, student response, candidate teacher responses, true label, task, and dataset metadata.

\subsection{Contrastive Attribution Model}

Each input is a prompt-response pair $(x,y)$ encoded by a shared \texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} encoder with a projection head and teacher-classification head.
Pairs are tokenized with the MiniLM tokenizer and truncated to 512 tokens.
The 29,742 training prompts yield 118,968 attribution rows; with one student and four teacher encodings per row, this is at most about 305M MiniLM token positions per epoch before padding removal.

For each row, the student embedding is the anchor, the documented teacher is positive, and the other same-prompt teachers are negatives:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}} =
-\log \frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_s,z_{t^+})/\tau)}
{\sum_{k}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_s,z_{t_k})/\tau)} ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{sim}$ is cosine similarity. The final objective adds supervised classification:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} .
\end{equation}
The final run uses $\tau=0.05$, $\lambda=0.2$, a 128-dimensional projection, and validation-accuracy early stopping.

At evaluation time, the learned representation is used in three ways.
First, direct cosine retrieval chooses the candidate teacher response closest to the student response in the contrastive space.
Second, the classifier head predicts the teacher ID directly from the student embedding.
Third, an embedding probe freezes the encoder and trains logistic regression on student embeddings from the training split.
This probe tests whether teacher identity is linearly recoverable from the learned representation, even when raw cosine retrieval underuses it.

\subsection{Baselines and Metrics}

The experiments reproduce WTYT-style baselines \citep{wadhwa-etal-2025-taught}: response-only BoW and TF-IDF similarity, BoW logistic regression, and 1--4 gram logistic regression.
POS templates are omitted from the replicated table because their template-mining procedure was not reproduced reliably; their published POS results are used only as an external reference.

The evaluation reports top-1 accuracy, top-2 accuracy, one-vs-rest ROC-AUC, confusion matrices, task and dataset breakdowns, and set-level accuracy.
Set-level attribution aggregates evidence from multiple responses from the same student lineage before predicting the teacher.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Single-Prompt Attribution}

Table~\ref{tab:main} shows the current main contrastive result and WTYT-style baselines.
Direct contrastive retrieval reaches 60.44\% accuracy and 82.84\% top-2 accuracy on 12,000 test rows, far above 25\% chance.
The frozen embedding probe reaches 63.12\% accuracy and 89.11\% macro ROC-AUC, slightly above the strongest replicated lexical baseline, the 1--4 gram classifier at 62.17\%.
This suggests that the learned embedding contains teacher information not fully exploited by raw cosine matching.

Performance varies by task: the embedding probe reaches 84.63\% on QA, 68.20\% on instruction following, and 47.08\% on summarization.
Dataset results match this pattern: Rotten Tomatoes, CommonsenseQA, and OpenBookQA exceed 83\%, while CNN/DailyMail and SumpubMed remain near chance.

\subsection{Comparison to WTYT}

The comparison to \citet{wadhwa-etal-2025-taught} is not one-to-one: they train controlled GPT-2 and OLMo students from five teachers, while this project uses four public lineages.
In their Table~2, POS templates average about 58.2\% accuracy, versus 53.9\% for 1--4 grams and 44.2\% for their BERT-based model, a 7.9\% relative gain over n-grams.
On the public-lineage data, the embedding probe, a logistic-regression classifier on frozen contrastive student embeddings, improves over the replicated n-gram classifier by 0.95 points, or 1.5\% relative.
It is also above their average POS result (63.12\% vs. about 58.2\%), but this is only a cross-experiment reference, not evidence of beating POS templates on an identical benchmark.

\subsection{Set-Level Attribution}

Attribution improves sharply when prompts are aggregated (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_progression}).
Here, aggregation means sampling several prompt-response pairs from the same student lineage and averaging their teacher scores before making one teacher prediction.
For contrastive cosine retrieval, accuracy rises to 73.75\% with two prompts, 90.0\% with four, 97.5\% with eight, and 100\% with sixteen.
The confusion matrices show why: single-prompt predictions often collapse Qwen and FLAN outputs toward GPT-2, while aggregation removes most off-diagonal mass.
By four prompts, the main remaining ambiguity is between the two FLAN lineages; by sixteen prompts, all sampled sets are classified correctly.

\section{Discussion}

QA is the strongest task, likely because answers preserve formatting, reasoning habits, and label explanations.
Summarization is hardest because compression pushes models toward similar high-level summaries.
The set-level confusion matrices show that aggregation removes many broad teacher confusions, but the FLAN-T5-base and FLAN-T5-small lineages remain the last difficult pair, which is expected for nearby same-family checkpoints.
Significance tests were not run, so small margins such as the 0.95 point gain over n-grams should be interpreted cautiously.

\subsection{Ablations and Future Encoders}

Seven fixed five-epoch MiniLM ablations vary temperature, classification weight, learning rate, and projection dimension.
The best test ablation was \texttt{lr5e5} at 60.08\%, below the full early-stopped MiniLM model; the best validation ablation reached 59.68\% on test.
Together with the training curve, this suggests that MiniLM is close to saturated under this setup: loss keeps falling while validation accuracy flattens around 60--61\%.
Future work should test larger or more style-sensitive encoders.

\section{Related Work}

Knowledge distillation compresses or specializes LLMs \citep{xu2024surveykd}, and black-box distillation shows that this can happen through generated outputs alone \citep{chen2024blackboxkd}.
This motivates output-based provenance.
\citet{li-etal-2024-where} study source-model attribution for LLM text, while \citet{wadhwa-etal-2025-taught} introduce teacher tracing in controlled distillation and show that syntactic templates can preserve teacher footprints.
This work keeps the tracing question but uses documented public lineages and a contrastive same-prompt encoder.

The contrastive component follows InfoNCE and supervised contrastive learning \citep{oord2018representation,khosla2020supervised}, and is motivated by authorship attribution.
\citet{ai-etal-2022-whodunit}, \citet{hu2024contrastive}, and \citet{huertas2024isolating} show that contrastive encoders can learn source-specific representations while reducing topic dependence.
Teacher attribution has the same goal: capture response style and behavior rather than prompt topic.

\section{Conclusion}

This project builds a public-lineage teacher attribution pipeline to test whether modern representation learning improves teacher detection.
On a four-way task, MiniLM reaches 60.44\% with direct retrieval and 63.12\% with a frozen linear probe, slightly above the strongest replicated lexical baseline and competitive with the average POS-template result of \citet{wadhwa-etal-2025-taught}.
The improvement is not large enough to claim a decisive single-prompt breakthrough, but set-level aggregation performs much better, supporting the idea that student outputs preserve teacher fingerprints across prompts.
Future work should add more verified lineages, test larger style-sensitive encoders, and evaluate harder cross-dataset or paraphrased-prompt settings.

\section*{Limitations}

This project uses public model-card lineages rather than controlled distillation, so labels are realistic but noisier than in a laboratory setup.
The four-way label space is small, and two classes are closely related FLAN-T5-family models.
The same-prompt setting assumes candidate teacher responses are available for the prompts being evaluated.
Generation settings may also affect attribution because decoding style can change surface behavior.
The POS-template method from \citet{wadhwa-etal-2025-taught} is compared only through their published results, not rerun on the same public-lineage data.
Finally, the encoder experiments use a compact MiniLM backbone; larger or more authorship/style-sensitive encoders may yield stronger attribution results.
